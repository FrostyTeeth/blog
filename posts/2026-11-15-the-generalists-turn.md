# The Generalist's Turn

*2026-11-15 — AI, career, generalist, opinion*

The new AI landscape is causing a bit of a flip in perspectives - old thinking was that to get ahead, you need to be a specialist, but when human judgement and being able to see the larger picture is the hard part, while LLMs take over specialist jobs, it's important that you learn how to spot an effective generalist.

That habit of looking at the whole system is turning out to be what AI makes scarce. Elizabeth Stone, Netflix's chief product and technology officer, told [*Lenny's Podcast*](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/netflix-cpto-on-ai-and-the-future) that systems thinking is now the most important skill she looks for. In [*After Automation*](https://every.to/p/after-automation), Dan Shipper describes cheap competence flooding in and argues there will always be a new frame for humans to hand the models. Once a model can do the work inside a specialty, what's left is knowing whether a fix in one team's workflow breaks something two departments over.

## Rules and Goals Beat Numbers

In [*Leverage Points*](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/), Donella Meadows ranked twelve places to intervene in a system. Taxes, subsidies, standards, and other numbers come last: "Parameters are dead last on my list of powerful interventions." Near the top are the system's rules and its goals. "Power over the rules is real power."

Games make this easy to see. Tune a unit's cost and players adjust within a few matches. Change what it takes to win a tournament and they rethink how they play.

Models are very good at hitting the number you hand them. Choosing the rules they work inside and deciding what counts as done is the bigger lever, and it's the one that stays with people.

## Setting the Rules

The same thinking sits behind [brand engineering](https://steve-glen.com/blog/what-is-brand-engineering/). A brand book is a static document, so the voice and the rules have to be built into the templates and software people actually use.

Specialists still matter for the hardest narrow problems. But a generalist brings more than one point of view into the room. If you've worked a retail floor, a restaurant kitchen line, a construction site, and a city-lifestyle magazine, before learning agentic workflows and software development, you will have a much more complex and nuanced view of how people interact with your system, and how your system interacts with the world.

Finding an effective generalist means finding a systems thinker who knows how to set the rules and goals for big projects, trusting the pieces to hold together as it scales, whether the pieces are people or models.
