# The Customer With Cash in Hand

*2026-10-25 — marketing, retail, customer-value, opinion*

I was in Simons department store this weekend. I like department stores because there is so much variety: it's a good place to casually shop for plain clothes, funky clothes, trendy clothes, semi-formal clothes. I get the sense that this is what Hudson's Bay wanted to be. But they went bankrupt and closed all their stores.

The Bay failed because they couldn't get it right. I worked at the Bay and I know why: they didn't create value for their customers. They failed at the most basic part of marketing: the value proposition. It's a simple trade, money for a product the customer wants more than the money. The Bay had the trade sitting on the counter and asked for a credit card instead. I worked there for about a year, 2018–2019, at the location in West Edmonton Mall, so it was busy. I started in menswear. From my personality type and my work experience, I was really good at generating sales. I worked hard and did my best to get the most clothes to the most customers who would be happy with their purchases. But generating sales was not the goal of the Bay frontline staff, our goal was to get credit card signups.

Customers hated these cards. There would be technical problems and there wasn't anything the staff at the Bay could do, the customer would have to call the Bay's Mastercard support and be on hold for hours, and often their issues weren't resolved. The only value I ever saw a customer get from the card was an extra discount on an item. And the Bay could have given loyal customers that discount a dozen other ways. It didn't have to come through a credit card.

I'm confident my sales were near the top of that floor. Yet, I was written up for not getting enough credit card signups.

I had recently finished my BCom, with a focus on marketing, strategy, organizational management, entrepreneurship. But I was burnt out and needed an easy and familiar job like retail sales. My education was still fresh in my mind, so I was also acutely aware that the Bay was consistently working against the customers. I would have been able to tell without my degree, but this time I had the language, the models and frameworks, and the knowledge that this stuff is teachable, and that anyone in management SHOULD know what the fuck they are doing. But no, upper management of the Bay was lost. They were fixed on one thing only, getting credit card signups.

Let me be clear: the culture was that a customer standing at the cash register with items in one hand and cash in the other, was a lower priority than somebody who might sign up for a Bay credit card. I guess the theory was that if they were Bay credit card holders, they would shop more often at the Bay, and the Bay would get some extra kickbacks from the credit card company. That's backwards though, just take the person who is ready to spend real dollars today as the priority. That's the goal — the company makes money and the customer leaves happy.

[![](images/hudsons-bay-comments.png)](https://www.reddit.com/r/vancouver/comments/of1hpe/hudsons_bay/)

Saturday, I walked out of Simons with two solid-colour t-shirts, inexpensive with good cotton, a textured long-sleeve that works for the office or a non-casual date, and a funky semi-furry striped sweater in an ugly colour that is soft, warm, and great for being weird. Nobody tried to sign me up for anything. They had the clothes I wanted, I had the money, and they took it. That's the whole job. The store is clean, the staff are friendly and genuine, and I'll be back.
